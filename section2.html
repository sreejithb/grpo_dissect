Are GRPO and Dr. GRPO actually reinforcement learning?

Both are routinely called RL algorithms. This page does not take that on trust. It writes down what reinforcement learning asks for, checks each method against the list, and then explains why the parts that are missing do not seem to matter for language models.

A one-paragraph recap, so this page stands alone. Both methods train a language model the same way. Give it a prompt, sample a group of \(G\) responses, and have a grader score each one. A response's learning signal is its score minus the group's average score. Responses with a positive signal are made more likely and the rest less likely. GRPO also divides the signal by the standard deviation of the group's scores and by the response's length. Dr. GRPO does neither.

\[ \text{Dr. GRPO:}\;\; \widehat{\nabla J} = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(r_i-\bar r)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_i) \]
\[ \text{GRPO:}\;\; \widehat{\nabla J} = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{r_i-\bar r}{\sigma\,|o_i|}\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_i) \]

Here \(r_i\) is the score of response \(o_i\), \(\bar r\) is the group mean, and \(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_i)=\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})\) is the direction in weight space that makes \(o_i\) more likely. Part 1 derives both.

1What makes something an RL algorithm?

Reinforcement learning is learning from consequences. Something acts, the world responds, and a score says how well things went. No one supplies the right answer. Press play to watch the loop that every textbook definition is built around.

That loop gives a checklist. The first seven items are the vocabulary. The eighth is the problem classical RL spends most of its effort on.

  1. AgentThe thing that chooses. Its rule for choosing is the policy: given what it sees, a probability for each possible action.
  2. EnvironmentEverything outside the agent. It responds to actions.
  3. StateWhat the agent sees before each choice. It changes as a consequence of earlier actions.
  4. ActionOne choice.
  5. RewardA number from the environment saying how good things are.
  6. ReturnThe total reward over an episode. This is what the agent tries to make large.
  7. Policy optimisationChanging the policy, from experience alone, so that the expected return goes up.
  8. Credit over timeWorking out which of many actions earned a return that arrived later. Usually done with a learned predictor of future reward, called a critic.

The same list, as the formal object

The standard formalisation is a Markov decision process. It has states \(s\), actions \(a\), a transition rule \(P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\) that belongs to the environment, and a reward \(r_t\). The agent's policy is \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\). The return from step \(t\) and the objective are

\[ R_t = \sum_{k\ge t} r_k, \qquad J(\theta) = \E_{\pi_\theta}\big[R_0\big] \]

Policy optimisation follows the gradient of \(J\), which the policy-gradient theorem writes as a sum over steps:

\[ \nabla J \;=\; \E\Big[\sum_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;A(s_t,a_t)\Big], \qquad A(s_t,a_t) = Q(s_t,a_t) - V(s_t) \]

\(V(s)\) is the expected return from state \(s\). \(Q(s,a)\) is the expected return after taking action \(a\) there. Their difference, the advantage, says how much better action \(a\) was than the policy's average at that step. "Credit over time" means estimating a separate \(A(s_t,a_t)\) for every \(t\).

Takeaway

RL is a loop: act, see the world respond, get scored, adjust. Formally: an MDP, a return, and a gradient that weights each step's action by its own advantage \(A(s_t,a_t)\).

2GRPO against the checklist

First, write language-model training in the formal terms. For a prompt \(q\) and a response \(o=(o_1,\dots,o_T)\):

MDP elementIn generalIn GRPO and Dr. GRPO
State\(s_t\)\(s_t=(q,\,o_{<t})\): the prompt plus the tokens so far
Action\(a_t\)\(a_t=o_t\): the next token
Transition\(s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)\), random, owned by the environment\(s_{t+1}=(s_t,\,a_t)\): append the token. Deterministic.
Reward\(r_t\) at every step\(r_t=0\) for \(t<T\); \(r_T=r(q,o)\) from the grader
Return\(R_t=\sum_{k\ge t}r_k\), different for each \(t\)\(R_t=r(q,o)\) for every \(t\)
Advantage used\(\hat A_t\approx Q(s_t,a_t)-V(s_t)\), one per step, from a critic\(\hat A_t=r(q,o)-\bar r\) for every \(t\) (GRPO: divided by \(\sigma\) and \(T\))

The right-hand column is a valid MDP, so the theorem applies. The verdicts below record how much of the general machinery that column actually exercises. Each item gets one of three verdicts. Holds means it is present in the ordinary sense. Weakened means it is present in a thinner form. Gone means it has been removed or replaced.

  1. AgentHoldsThe language model is the policy: given the text so far, a probability for each next token.
  2. EnvironmentWeakenedIt is a set of prompts and a grader. It speaks twice per episode: once to hand over the prompt and once to give a score. In between it does nothing.
  3. StateWeakenedThe state is the prompt plus the tokens written so far. It does change, but only because the model appends its own token. The agent writes its own next state, and the world never surprises it mid-episode.
  4. ActionHoldsWith an ambiguity. An action is either one token (hundreds per episode) or the whole response (one per episode). GRPO computes its update token by token and judges response by response.
  5. RewardHoldsOne number from the grader, at the end.
  6. ReturnWeakenedWith a single reward, \(R_t=r(q,o)\) for all \(t\). Every token in the response inherits the same number.
  7. Policy optimisationWeakenedIt learns by trial and error and does improve the policy. But the factors \(1/\sigma\) and \(1/|o_i|\) mean \(\E[\widehat{\nabla J}]\neq c\,\nabla J\) for any constant \(c\). It climbs a slightly different hill (part 1 has the derivation).
  8. Credit over timeGoneNo critic and no per-step \(\hat A_t\). The group mean \(\bar r\) estimates only \(V(s_0)\), the value of the prompt, and is reused at every step. In its place: ask the same prompt \(G\) times and compare the outcomes. That needs something classical RL cannot usually assume, the power to restart from the identical state whenever you like.

GRPO keeps the agent, the actions, the reward and trial-and-error learning. It thins out the environment, the state and the return. It drops credit assignment over time and replaces it with comparison inside a group.

3Dr. GRPO against the checklist

Dr. GRPO collects experience exactly as GRPO does: same prompts, same groups, same grader. So its verdicts on the environment side come out the same, for the same reasons. The one row where it differs is the row about what the update optimises.

  1. AgentHoldsThe language model.
  2. EnvironmentWeakenedPrompts and a grader. Silent between the prompt and the score.
  3. StateWeakenedPrompt plus tokens so far, written by the agent itself.
  4. ActionHoldsToken or whole response, with the same ambiguity.
  5. RewardHoldsOne number at the end.
  6. ReturnWeakened\(R_t=r(q,o)\) for all \(t\). The same number reaches every token.
  7. Policy optimisationHolds\(\E[\widehat{\nabla J}]=\tfrac{G-1}{G}\nabla J\): the true gradient of expected return, shortened by a constant. This is the textbook policy-gradient method with a baseline, wrapped in a limit on how far one update may move the policy.
  8. Credit over timeGoneNo critic. \(\hat A_t=r-\bar r\) for all \(t\), with the same need for restarts.

Takeaway

Dr. GRPO is the cleaner of the two as an optimiser: it climbs the hill RL says it should. As a user of the RL loop it is exactly as thin as GRPO.

4Which environments suit them?

Suppose we accept both as RL methods. The checklist then predicts where they should do well: wherever the weakened and missing items were not needed in the first place.

5If this is not classical RL, why does it work for language models?

Because training a language model on graded outputs is not a classical RL problem either. Play both lanes and count how often the environment speaks.

The world does not answer back. Classical machinery exists to cope with an environment that reacts: a state the agent cannot predict, consequences that unfold over time. While a model writes a response, the only thing changing the state is the model. There are no outside dynamics to cope with.

The reward is about the outcome. The grader scores the finished response. It has no opinion about token 37. Because the transition is deterministic and the only reward is terminal, the sum over steps collapses: \(\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_t\mid s_t) = \nabla\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)\), and the policy gradient becomes \(\E\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)\,(r(q,o)-V(q))\big]\). That is a statement about whole responses. Giving every token the same number is what the mathematics asks for here, not a shortcut.

Restarts are free. A critic is a way of guessing \(V(s)\) when you can visit \(s\) only once. A prompt can be visited as many times as you like. Sampling \(G\) responses and averaging their scores measures the value directly, \(\bar r \to V(s_0)=\E_{o\sim\pi}[r(q,o)]\), with no second network to train.

Exploration is already done. Classical agents begin by acting at random. A pretrained model begins by writing plausible responses, some right and some wrong. That mix is what makes within-group comparison informative from the first step.

Some credit still gets through. Within one response every token gets the same signal. Across thousands of groups, though, a token pattern that keeps turning up in above-average responses collects positive signal again and again, while incidental tokens average out. Credit assignment happens slowly, by statistics. This is limited, and it is the first thing to fail when episodes become long.

Deeper mathematical note what a critic would add

In the theorem, \(A(s_t,a_t)\) may be replaced by \(Q(s_t,a_t)-b(s_t)\) for any baseline \(b\) that does not depend on \(a_t\). Both methods replace \(Q(s_t,a_t)\) with the one observed outcome \(r(q,o)\) for every \(t\), and replace \(b(s_t)\) with the group mean, an estimate of the value of the first state \(s_0=q\) only. Both replacements are unbiased (Dr. GRPO) and are what a critic-free Monte Carlo policy gradient does. A critic would instead estimate \(V(s_t)\) for each prefix; that function exists, but learning it means training a second large network on sparse signal.

Takeaway

The full machinery of RL answers problems that graded text generation mostly does not pose: reacting environments, unrepeatable states, rewards tied to moments. Remove the problems and the simpler method is enough.

6Four answers

Are GRPO and Dr. GRPO valid RL algorithms?

Yes. Both learn a policy from sampled experience and a reward, with no supervision about the right action. That is the definition.

In what sense?

They are policy-gradient methods of the simplest kind: sample complete episodes, score them, and shift probability towards the better ones. Dr. GRPO does this without bias. GRPO does it with two known distortions.

In what sense are they not classical RL?

They have no critic, no per-step credit, and no way to handle an environment that reacts during an episode. They also rely on restarting from the same state at will. They solve the corner of RL where the environment only poses a question and grades the answer.

Why do they still work for language models?

Because that corner is where language-model training lives. The environment is silent mid-episode, the reward describes the whole output, restarts cost nothing, and pretraining has already handled exploration.
