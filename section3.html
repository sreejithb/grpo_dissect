Can GRPO and Dr. GRPO be used on classic RL problems?

Both methods were built for language models. This page translates them into the terms of classic reinforcement learning, works out what is lost in translation, turns that into a prediction about where they should do well, and then checks the prediction against published experiments.

1The recipe, translated

Nothing in either method is specific to text. Each piece has a counterpart in a classic problem such as balancing a pole or planning a route.

In language-model trainingIn a classic RL problem
Prompt \(q\)Start state \(s_0\) (or a problem instance, such as one map of cities)
Response \(o_i\)Trajectory \(\tau_i=(s_0,a_{i,0},s_{i,1},a_{i,1},\dots)\): one full episode, also called a rollout
Token \(o_{i,t}\)Action \(a_{i,t}\)
Score \(r_i\)Return \(R_i=\sum_{t} r_{i,t}\): the episode's total reward
Group of \(G\) responses to one prompt\(G\) rollouts from the same start state
Group mean \(\bar r\)Group mean return \(\bar R=\frac1G\sum_j R_j\)

Substituting these into Dr. GRPO's estimator from part 1 gives the classic-RL version:

\[ \widehat{\nabla J} \;=\; \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\,\big(R_i-\bar R\big)\,\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\big(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t}\big) \]

In words: run the episode \(G\) times from the same start, compare each total to the group's average, and push every action in an episode up or down by that one difference.

The requirement hiding in the table

The last-but-one row asks for \(G\) rollouts from the same start state. With a prompt that is free. In a classic problem it needs a simulator that can be reset to an exact state.

Switch the figure to "Different start states" to see why it matters. Some starts are easier than others, so returns differ before any action is taken. \(\bar R\) then estimates the average over starts, not the value \(V(s_0)\) of this start. The method is still unbiased, since \(\bar R\) is a legal baseline by part 1's argument. But \(R_i-\bar R\) now mostly measures which start was lucky, which is the off-centre problem from part 1.

Takeaway

To apply the method: reset a simulator to one start state, run \(G\) episodes, and weight every action of episode \(i\) by \(R_i-\bar R\). It needs a resettable environment and nothing else.

2It is always valid. The question is noise.

Why it stays unbiased

The probability of a trajectory multiplies the policy's choices by the environment's responses:

\[ p_\theta(\tau) \;=\; \prod_t \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t) \]

Take the logarithm and differentiate with respect to \(\theta\). The environment's factors \(P\) do not contain \(\theta\), so they vanish, and the score function of a trajectory is \(g(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\). That is the same form as the score function of a response. Part 1's proof never used anything else, so it carries over with \(o\) replaced by \(\tau\):

\[ \E\big[\widehat{\nabla J}\big] \;=\; \frac{G-1}{G}\,\nabla J \]

This holds for any environment: random transitions, long horizons, rewards at every step. The estimator never points the wrong way on average. So the question for classic RL is not "is it correct?" but "how many samples does it need?"

What the single number contains

The ideal weight for the action at step \(t\) is its own advantage, \(A(s_t,a_t)=Q(s_t,a_t)-V(s_t)\): how much better that action was than the policy's average at that state. The weight actually used is \(R-\bar R\approx R-V(s_0)\). Write the return as a sum of per-step changes in value (the sum telescopes, with the value after the last step equal to zero):

\[ R - V(s_0) \;=\; \sum_{k}\Big[\,r_k + V(s_{k+1}) - V(s_k)\,\Big] \]

Each bracket splits into the advantage of the action taken, plus whatever the environment added by chance: \(\varepsilon_k = r_k+V(s_{k+1})-Q(s_k,a_k)\), which averages to zero and is exactly zero when transitions are deterministic. So, for the action at step \(t\):

\[ R - V(s_0) \;=\; \underbrace{A(s_t,a_t)}_{\text{what step } t \text{ deserves}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{k\neq t}A(s_k,a_k)}_{\text{every other decision}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{k}\varepsilon_k}_{\text{the environment's luck}} \]

Only the first term is about step \(t\). The other two are noise as far as that step is concerned. If the terms are of similar size and roughly independent, then for an episode of \(T\) steps the first term is one part in \(T(1+\lambda)\) of the weight's variance, where \(\lambda\) is the size of the luck relative to the advantages.

Does this not apply to language models too? Yes. A 500-token response has \(T=500\) in this formula, and each token's own credit is a small part of \(r_i-\bar r\). Part 2 called this credit assignment "by statistics". Two things soften it there: \(\lambda=0\), because nothing outside the model acts mid-response, and a pretrained model starts with most of its choices already good.

Takeaway

On any problem the update is right on average. Its weight for each action is that action's advantage, buried under every other action's advantage and all of the environment's luck. The burial gets deeper with horizon and with randomness.

3A test bed

The claim can be tested on a problem small enough to run in this page. An episode has \(T\) steps. At each step the policy picks one of two actions; the good one pays 1 and the other pays 0, plus random noise of size \(s\) added by the environment. The policy starts at 50/50 on every step. Three ways of weighting the action at step \(t\) are compared, all using groups of \(G=8\) rollouts and the same learning rate:

MethodWeight on the action at step \(t\)What it stands for
Episode return\(R_i-\bar R\)GRPO and Dr. GRPO as translated above
Reward-to-go\(\sum_{k\ge t} r_{i,k}\) minus its group mean at step \(t\)Drop rewards that came before the action. Still no critic.
Per-step\(r_{i,t}\) minus its group mean at step \(t\)What a perfect critic would give in this problem

Three things to try. Set \(T=1\): the curves coincide, because one decision followed by one score is where the method has nothing to lose. Raise \(T\) with \(s=0\): the episode-return curve falls behind, by the dilution in the formula. Then raise \(s\): it falls further, and reward-to-go recovers only part of the gap, because future luck is still in its weight.

Untick the box to use raw weights, as Dr. GRPO does. All three then have the same expected update, and the gap shrinks when \(s=0\). It reopens as soon as the environment is noisy.

A caution about the test bed. Its steps do not interact, so it isolates dilution and nothing else. And "per-step" assumes a perfect critic, which real critics are not. It shows the direction of the effect, not its size on a real problem.

Takeaway

With one decision per episode the simple method loses nothing. Its handicap grows with the number of decisions and with the environment's randomness, as the decomposition predicts.

4The hypothesis

The decomposition and the test bed suggest two requirements and three dials.

Requirement: resets. The environment can be put back in the same start state \(G\) times. Without this there is no group.

Requirement: some early success. If all \(G\) rollouts get the same return, every weight is zero. A random starting policy on a hard puzzle fails every time and learns nothing. Language models avoid this by being pretrained.

Dial 1: randomness of the environment. The more the world's responses vary, the larger the luck term.

Dial 2: horizon. The more decisions share one return, the smaller each one's share.

Dial 3: where the reward arrives. If reward comes only at the end, a critic has little to work with either, so going without one costs little. If reward comes at every step, the timing is information, and summing it into one total throws that away.

So the prediction is: these methods should be closest to the best available method on resettable, deterministic problems with a single outcome score, and furthest behind on long, noisy problems with reward at every step. The map places some well-known problems. Click one for the reasoning.

Takeaway

Predicted best: routing and other one-shot construction problems. Predicted worst: long-horizon control with dense reward, games against an opponent, and anything that cannot be reset.

5What published experiments show

The hypothesis above is reasoning, not results. Three published lines of work bear on it. I have read their abstracts and summaries, not reproduced them.

ProblemPredictionWhat was reported
Routing: travelling salesman, vehicle routing, knapsackGood fitSupports POMO (Kwon et al., 2020) trains with exactly this baseline: the mean return over several rollouts of the same instance, with no critic. It reported the strongest learned-solver results of its time on all three problems. One difference: its rollouts are forced to begin from different first moves, so they are not independent samples.
CartPoleMiddlingBetter than predicted De Oliveira et al. (2025) found critic-free methods competitive with PPO here, and only here. Their explanation is early termination: the episode ends when the pole falls, so the return says directly how long the policy survived.
Acrobot, MountainCar (continuous), HalfCheetah, HumanoidPoor fitSupports The same study found GRPO well behind PPO on all of these, and no better than a simple batch-mean baseline. It concludes that learned critics remain necessary for long-horizon tasks.
Dense-reward robot control, with returns in place of episode totalsImproves with reward-to-goSupports GR2PO (2026) keeps the method critic-free but compares discounted returns across parallel rollouts at each time step. It reports competitive control performance on the tasks it tested, with PPO as the main comparison.

What the hypothesis missed

Early termination. I rated CartPole as middling because of its horizon and per-step reward. The experiments say it is the one classic control task where the simple method keeps up. When an episode ends at the first serious mistake, the return itself locates the mistake in time, and that does part of a critic's job. This deserves to be a fourth dial.

Group construction. The same study found that smaller groups did better than larger ones, and suggests the reason is that its groups mixed unrelated episodes. That is the "different start states" setting of the first figure. If so, that study did not test the method with what the language-model setting gets for free: many rollouts from an identical start. That version remains to be measured on these benchmarks.

Takeaway

The evidence so far agrees with the prediction at both ends: strong on routing problems, weak on long-horizon control. It adds one thing the reasoning missed: early termination helps a great deal.

6If the fit is poor: what to add, in order

The gap between Dr. GRPO and a full method such as PPO can be closed one step at a time. Each step removes one term from the noise in section 2.

StepWeight on the action at step \(t\)RemovesCosts
0. Dr. GRPO\(R_i-\bar R\)The shared part of the returnResets
1. Reward-to-go\(\sum_{k\ge t}r_{i,k}\) minus its group mean at step \(t\)Rewards and luck from before the actionNothing more
2. A learned baseline\(\sum_{k\ge t}r_{i,k}-V_\phi(s_{i,t})\)The need for a shared start state; differences between statesTraining a critic \(V_\phi\)
3. Bootstrapping (PPO with GAE)\(r_{i,t}+V_\phi(s_{i,t+1})-V_\phi(s_{i,t})\), blended over a few stepsMost of the later decisions and later luckSome bias from the critic's errors

Step 1 is free and stays unbiased: the group mean of reward-to-go at step \(t\), leaving out rollout \(i\), does not depend on rollout \(i\)'s actions, so part 1's condition holds. Steps 2 and 3 are the "credit over time" machinery that part 2 found missing.

Which variant to start from

Start from Dr. GRPO, not GRPO. Part 1 showed that GRPO divides each weight by the episode's length. In classic problems length often is the outcome. In CartPole a long episode is a good one, so dividing by length shrinks the push on exactly the episodes that did best. Dividing by the spread \(\sigma\) is less harmful, and is close to the advantage normalisation that PPO implementations already use.

Takeaway

Dr. GRPO is the bottom rung of a ladder whose top is PPO. Climb only as far as the problem forces you to: reward-to-go first, a critic when the horizon or the noise demands it.

7Four answers

Can they be applied to classic RL problems?

Yes, to any problem with a resettable simulator. Run \(G\) episodes from one start state and weight every action by \(R_i-\bar R\). The update is unbiased on any environment.

Where should they work best?

Where one decision, or one uninterrupted construction, is followed by one score, and the environment adds no randomness. Routing problems are the clearest case, and the published results there are strong.

Where should they work worst?

Long episodes with reward at every step, random environments, opponents, and anything that cannot be reset. On standard control benchmarks beyond CartPole they have been measured well behind PPO.

What decides it?

How much of \(R_i-\bar R\) is about the action being weighted. That share falls with horizon and with randomness, and rises when episodes end at the first mistake.
