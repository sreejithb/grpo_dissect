The bandit analogy, and why it explains LLM training

There is a much older and much simpler problem than reinforcement learning hiding inside GRPO. Once you see it, the algorithm stops looking like a trimmed-down version of something bigger and starts looking like the obvious thing to do.

1A puzzle

A model is asked the same question four times and gives four different answers. A judge scores each one from 0 to 10.

You probably did three things without thinking about them. You compared the answers to each other: a 7 is good here only because the group averaged 5.5. You treated each answer as one whole thing that was either worth repeating or not. And you never asked what happens after the answer, because nothing does. The question was asked, an answer was given, a score came back, and that was the end.

Takeaway

One situation, several complete attempts, one score each. Learning means shifting probability towards each attempt in proportion to how far it beat the group average.

2The same puzzle, in a casino

This puzzle has a name that is about seventy years old. Picture a row of slot machines. Each pays out a different amount on average, with some randomness on top. You pull one lever, you see one payout, and the round is over. Your only job is to work out which lever to favour.

Slot machines used to be called one-armed bandits, so this is the bandit problem. Each lever is an arm.

Now put machines in two rooms, with different payouts in each. Before choosing, you are told which room you are in. That piece of information is the context, and this version is a contextual bandit. Let the learner below play.

The learner keeps a preference \(H_x(a)\) for each arm in each room, and \(\pi(a\mid x)\propto e^{H_x(a)}\). After pulling arm \(a\) and receiving \(r\), it updates every arm \(k\) in that room:

\[ H_x(k) \;\leftarrow\; H_x(k) + \alpha\,\big(r-\bar r_x\big)\,\big(\mathbb{1}[k=a]-\pi(k\mid x)\big) \]

The last bracket is \(\partial\log\pi(a\mid x)/\partial H_x(k)\). So this is \(H\leftarrow H+\alpha\,(r-b)\,\nabla\log\pi(a\mid x)\) with baseline \(b=\bar r_x\): a reward minus a baseline, times the direction that makes the chosen arm more likely. It is the same update rule as in part 1.

Four ingredients, and no others:

Context. What you are shown before choosing. Action. The one arm you pull. Reward. The one payout you get. Policy. Your rule for choosing an arm in each context, improved a little after every reward.

What is missing matters as much. Pulling a lever does not change the room. It does not change which room you see next. No choice has consequences beyond its own payout.

Written as an objective, a contextual bandit maximises

\[ J(\theta) \;=\; \E_{x}\;\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,r(x,a)\,\big] \]

with value \(V(x)=\E_{a\sim\pi}[r(x,a)]\) and advantage \(A(x,a)=r(x,a)-V(x)\). There is no sum over time anywhere in it.

Takeaway

A contextual bandit is: see a context \(x\), make one choice \(a\), get one reward \(r(x,a)\), done. The policy improves by weighting each choice by \(r-V(x)\), the reward minus what is usual for that context.

3Mapping the casino onto a language model

Every piece has a counterpart. The prompt is the context. A complete generated response is one arm. The verifier's or judge's score is the payout. The language model is the policy. Substitute the names into the bandit objective and you get the objective of single-turn language-model training, symbol for symbol:

\[ \underbrace{\E_{x}\,\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,a)\big]}_{\text{contextual bandit}} \qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\E_{q}\,\E_{o\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid q)}\big[r(q,o)\big]}_{\text{graded response generation}} \]

So for this setting the word "analogy" undersells it. The response is produced token by token, but \(\pi_\theta(o\mid q)=\prod_t\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\) is still one probability for one choice, and its gradient is the sum of the token-level gradients. The tokens are the internal structure of pulling one lever.

Two things are different, and both shape the algorithm.

The number of arms is beyond counting. A room has three levers. A prompt has more possible responses than there are atoms in the universe. No learner can try each arm and keep a tally, so the policy has to generalise: reinforcing one response must also raise the chances of similar ones. A language model does this naturally, since all responses come from the same weights.

You can pull many arms in the same round. In a casino you pull one lever and the round is gone. With a model you can hand it the identical prompt eight times and get eight responses. That is the opening GRPO uses.

Takeaway

Prompt, response, score and model line up with context, arm, reward and policy. The differences are an astronomically large set of arms and the freedom to sample several of them per context.

4Why GRPO fits naturally

The casino learner had to keep a running average for each room, built up over many visits. A prompt may be seen only once in training, so there is no history to average. GRPO builds the average on the spot instead. Step through it.

Generate. Sample \(o_1,\dots,o_G\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid q)\): several arms pulled in the same context.

Score. Each response gets its payout \(r_i=r(q,o_i)\).

Compare. The group mean \(\bar r=\frac1G\sum_j r_j\) is a sample estimate of \(V(q)\), "what is usual for this context". So \(r_i-\bar r\) is a sample estimate of the bandit advantage \(A(q,o_i)=r(q,o_i)-V(q)\): how much better response \(i\) did than its siblings. Because everything is measured against the group, what matters is the ranking and the gaps within it, not the raw scores. A hard prompt where 0.3 is the best anyone managed still produces a clear winner.

Update. \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,\frac1G\sum_i (r_i-\bar r)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)\). Responses with a positive advantage are made more likely, the others less. Compare this with the casino update: it is the same rule, with \(\bar r\) computed from the group in place of the running average.

The tick-box shows the one place the two variants differ at this level. GRPO divides the advantages by the group's standard deviation \(\sigma\), giving \((r_i-\bar r)/\sigma\), so that every prompt produces weights of similar size. Dr. GRPO leaves them as they are. Part 1 looks at what that division costs.

Takeaway

GRPO is the casino learner's rule with one change: it gets its "usual payout" by sampling a group right now, not from memory. "Group relative" is a literal description.

5Why full RL may be unnecessary

Reinforcement learning in its full form is for problems where choices have consequences that unfold. A simple test separates the two kinds of problem: between the first choice and the final score, how many times does the world answer back?

In chess, every move is answered by an opponent, and a blunder on move 9 may only cost the game on move 40. A walking robot is answered by physics a thousand times a minute. Both need machinery for tracing a late result back to an early choice.

A model writing one response is answered once, at the end. From the world's side, the whole response is a single choice. In symbols, the probability of a full episode in general RL is

\[ p_\theta(\text{episode}) \;=\; \prod_t \underbrace{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}_{\text{learner}}\;\underbrace{P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)}_{\text{world}} \]

In chess and robotics the \(P\) factors are an opponent and physics, and they sit between the learner's choices. For a single response every \(P\) factor equals 1, because the next state is just the text so far. What remains is \(\prod_t\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})=\pi_\theta(o\mid q)\): one draw from the policy. The sequential problem and the bandit problem are then the same problem.

Takeaway

Much of language-model training has the shape of a contextual bandit: one context, one elaborate choice, one reward. A bandit-shaped problem only needs a bandit-shaped algorithm.

6Where the analogy fails

The analogy rests on one condition: the world stays quiet until the end. Drag the slider to break it.

Tool use. When a model calls a search engine or runs code, the result is not something it wrote. Its next choice depends on what came back. That is a state the world controls.

Multi-turn agents. A user's reply depends on what the model said. An early clumsy turn can sink a conversation ten turns later. This is the chess problem again.

Persistent memory. If what a model does today changes what it is shown tomorrow, contexts are no longer independent draws. The casino assumption that pulling a lever never changes the room is false.

Long-horizon reasoning. Even with a silent world, a response of fifty thousand tokens is a strain on "one arm". One number has to explain an enormous number of internal decisions, and the comparison within a group becomes very noisy.

In all four cases the \(P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\) factors are no longer 1, or the reward no longer depends on one choice. The estimator \(\frac1G\sum_i(r_i-\bar r)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})\) is still an unbiased policy gradient, with \(\bar r\) as a baseline for the starting state. But \(r_i-\bar r\) is no longer the advantage of any single choice. It is one number for the whole episode, and it now also contains the luck of what the world sent back.

In practice GRPO-style training is still applied in these settings, by treating the entire episode as a single arm. That is an approximation, and a rougher one as the number of interruptions grows. This is where critics, per-step credit and the rest of the RL toolkit come back in.

Takeaway

The bandit picture holds while an episode is one uninterrupted act followed by one score. Each time the world answers back mid-episode, it holds a little less.

7The whole story in one line

Why GRPO resembles a contextual-bandit algorithm

It sees a context (the prompt), pulls several arms (responses), reads one payout each (scores), and shifts its policy towards the arms that beat the group average. Nothing in it tracks what happens after an arm is pulled.

Why this helps explain its success

Graded text generation is a bandit-shaped problem. The parts of RL that GRPO leaves out are the parts built for consequences over time, and here there are few.

Where the analogy begins to fail

As soon as the world answers back before the final score: tools, multi-turn dialogue, memory, very long reasoning. The more of that there is, the more a single number per episode has to explain.
